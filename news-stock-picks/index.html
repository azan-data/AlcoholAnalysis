Can you trust this?

What decades of research say about predicting stocks, and how this page measures up

Use it to research, not to decide.

No method, including this one, reliably predicts where a single stock goes over the next few weeks. Most professional stock pickers fail to beat a plain index fund, and most published trading signals fade once people use them. What this page can do is gather cross-checked news in one place, show its reasoning, and lean on the few signals with real evidence behind them.

  • It has no track record. The model hasn't been tested on past data, and its first snapshot is one trading day old.
  • Prices are the latest close that two sources agree on, so they lag the live market.
  • Sentiment tags and the economic backdrop are Claude's judgment calls, shown so you can check them.
  • It covers 24 large stocks and knows nothing about your goals, taxes or timeline.

How this page's score measures up

The default Evidence-weighted style puts the most weight on the best-supported signals. Profitability, value, insider buying and estimate revisions have stronger evidence than news but aren't in the score yet; each needs per-company data collected and cross-checked first.

Using it sensibly

  • Keep the core of your money in a low-cost, diversified index fund. That's the benchmark these picks have to beat, and most professionals don't.
  • Treat a Buy as a lead to research. The strongest cases are stocks where several well-supported signals agree, like a strong price trend and solid profits.
  • Keep any single stock to a small share of what you invest.
  • Be careful right before earnings. Netflix's options, for example, price in about a 9% move on its report.
  • Trade less. The most active traders in one large study earned 11.4% a year while the market returned 17.9%.
  • Check live prices and news before acting. This page only changes when it's refreshed.

How the score works

Every input is shown under "Show the math" for each stock
Cross-checking
A story moves a score only when at least two independent outlets report it; copies of the same article on other sites count once. Single-source stories stay visible but are marked "Not counted". Headlines you paste count only after you confirm them. Closing prices were matched across at least two quote pages unless a stock says otherwise; 52-week ranges come from one quote page each.
News sentiment
Claude reads each story and tags which sectors and stocks it helps or hurts, from −3 to +3. The reasoning appears as the "Market read" on every story, so you can check it. A tag on the stock itself counts in full, a tag on its sector counts 60%, and a story's weight halves every 7 days. The total s becomes a 0–100 score, 50 + 50·tanh(s/3), shown as Bearish to Bullish.
Price trend
Where the price sits in its 52-week range: 0 at the low, 100 at the high. Stocks near their 52-week highs have tended to keep outperforming (George & Hwang, 2004).
Room to rebound
How far the price is below its 52-week high, with 35% or more scoring 100. This is the bargain-hunting bet, the opposite of Price trend: recent losers tend to bounce in the short run (Jegadeesh, 1990). Your style decides how much each counts.
Earnings
The latest quarter against analyst estimates: a big beat with raised guidance scores 100, a miss scores 25. Stocks that beat tend to keep drifting up for weeks (Bernard & Thomas, 1989). Left out for companies that haven't reported yet.
Economic backdrop
Claude's judgment of how each sector fits today's conditions: oil above $100, a 5% 10-year yield, 3.7% inflation, an active U.S.–Iran conflict and midterms on Nov 3. The reasoning is shown for each stock.
Risk
Volatility estimated from the 52-week range, σ ≈ ln(high/low) ÷ 1.6 per year, plus 15 points when earnings are due within 10 days. The penalty is risk × risk aversion × 0.3.

Score = weighted average of the available factors − risk penalty. Missing factors drop out and the other weights are rescaled. Buy ≥ , Lean buy ≥ , Hold ≥ , Avoid below.

Typical 3-month swing is one standard deviation, σ ÷ 2. About two times in three, a stock has moved less than this over three months.
